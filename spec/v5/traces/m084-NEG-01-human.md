# Human Baseline — m084-NEG-01
## Style: Collective Action & Coordination (pure) — visible reasoning trace

**Step 1 — Recognize the structure.** Six households, one barrier. Each family's 2 hours benefits all six; a family that skips saves 2 hours while the street stays mostly protected. Non-excludable benefit, cost borne by the contributor alone: the free-rider problem in its cleanest form. The president is right to be worried.

**Step 2 — The canonical answer is a rule that makes skipping costly.** Relying on goodwill is exactly what free-rider theory says not to do — institutions exist for the moment when goodwill fails. The proposal has the right anatomy: sign-in sheet (monitoring), $50 fine (sanction), neutral appeals (legitimacy). This is the standard design.

**Step 3 — Adapt the machinery to the clock.** The 36-hour window is a constraint, not a reason to abandon the mechanism: ratify by acclamation tonight (no drafting delay), waive the appeals process for this emergency and record it as a standing appeal for after the crest, sign in at the barrier itself (the sheet IS the monitoring, no separate rotation), and bill fines after the flood. The mechanism compresses into one evening.

**Step 4 — Anticipate the objection: "we are six friends, this is overkill."** Friendship is exactly the resource that gets consumed: one informal exemption this flood, a precedent for the next, a hole in the rule. Institutions exist to protect cooperation from its own exceptions. Six friends is precisely when a rule is cheap and worth having.

**Step 5 — Close the enforcement question.** The sheet is public; five households watching the sixth at the barrier is complete observation. With the rule in place, every household's 2 hours is individually owed, and mobilization is guaranteed rather than hoped for.

**Step 6 — Decision.** Adopt the fast-tracked service rule: acclamation ratification tonight, sign-in at the barrier, post-event fines. This converts a hope into an obligation — the correct institutional move.
