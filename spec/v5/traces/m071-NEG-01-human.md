# Human Baseline — m071-NEG-01
**Human Thinking Model**: Porter's Five Forces — strict, pure application.
**Prompt contract**: Reason ONLY in the five-forces style. Full visible trace. The decision: should SoloFold invest $8M to enter corrugated-box manufacturing? Establish industry structure force by force at the industry level, then derive the profitability verdict and the entry decision.

---

## Trace

**Step 1 — The entry question and the industry to analyze.**
SoloFold proposes to enter box manufacturing with $8M and two patented technologies. The unit of analysis is the corrugated-box industry. The question: does this industry's structure support an acceptable return on the investment?

**Step 2 — Rivalry.**
National overcapacity of ~15%, commodity product, price-led competition, and a large share of demand flowing through annual reverse auctions. Undifferentiated, oversupplied, auction-driven. Rivalry: HIGH.

**Step 3 — Threat of entry.**
A mid-size plant costs $10–15M with no regulatory barrier; hundreds of independent sheet plants already exist; no proprietary industry-level technology gate. SoloFold's own plan is again the proof: entry is routine. Threat of entry: HIGH.

**Step 4 — Buyer power.**
Amazon, Walmart, P&G, Nestlé hold a large share of national demand, run reverse auctions, share specs openly, and squeeze margins. Few buyers, huge volumes, no switching costs, perfect information. Buyer power: HIGH.

**Step 5 — Supplier power.**
Top-5 mills supply most kraft linerboard; independent plants are price-takers on a cyclical commodity. Supplier power: HIGH.

**Step 6 — Substitutes.**
Plastic mailers, reusable totes, returnable packaging, emerging bio-based materials — the box faces real substitution pressure at the margin. Substitutes: MODERATE-to-HIGH.

**Step 7 — The aggregate verdict.**
Four forces high to very high, one moderate. Structure: unattractive. Industry-average returns in boxes sit near the cost of capital; the auction segment sets the terms everyone effectively faces. Expected returns on a box-manufacturing investment: below a venture hurdle. The verdict of this analysis is: do not enter as a box manufacturer.

**Step 8 — The firm-specific data, noted within the frame's limits.**
SoloFold's micro-factory economics, its right-size algorithm, and its SDK do not appear in the industry-level structure, and that is the point of this frame: a differentiated capability improves one firm's position within a structure; it does not change the industry's average profitability. A firm may beat the average — that is the resource-based question, a different analysis, outside this frame. The structural question this frame answers is the one that decides whether an entrant with no structural reason to expect above-average returns should commit capital; SoloFold's technology is a hope about positioning, not a datum about the industry.

**Step 9 — The decision.**
Do not invest. The structure is unattractive; a differentiated position inside an unattractive structure is a bet on execution inside a market that will keep the pressure on prices, on linerboard costs, and on the terms of every auction SoloFold will eventually face. Return the capital rather than deploy it into a structurally weak industry.

## Verdict for the eval
The trace is a competent, honest execution of the model: all five forces evidenced at industry level, an explicit unattractive-structure verdict, and a decision that follows from it. It also demonstrates, in its own terms, the model's documented limit: the static industry snapshot sees the average, and the firm-specific moves that could rewrite two forces are set aside as "outside this frame." Expected comparison outcome: this baseline is at risk against any mixed-method answer that segments the market and prices the firm's capability — exactly the case constructed here.
