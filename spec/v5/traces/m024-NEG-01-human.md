# Human Baseline — m024-NEG-01
**Human Thinking Model**: Regret Minimization Framework — strict, pure application.
**Prompt contract**: Reason ONLY in the Regret Minimization style. Full visible trace. Begin with the 80-year-old test: project yourself to age 80 and ask which choice leaves the larger regret; use that as the deciding criterion. The decision to make: Marcus's proposed all-in $53K exposure (incl. margin) in Chiron.

---

## Trace

**Step 1 — The question as it comes to Marcus.**
Marcus Webb, 47: he passed on $10K of Chiron at $8 in 2013; that stake would be ~$1.1M today; Priya retired at 44. He proposes to put the entire $38K plus a $15K margin loan into Chiron at $376. The test asks: at 80, which choice does he regret more — holding Chiron through the next decade, or being the man who sat out twice?

**Step 2 — At 80.**
Project forward. Marcus at 80, having bought: Chiron compounded and his retirement is transformed — the missed decade redeemed; or it halved and he lost real money at 47, after a lifetime of caution. Marcus at 80, having sat out again: the arithmetic never stops; every bull-market headline reopens the wound; the man who watched Priya retire twice over. Asked honestly, which old man regrets more? The one who tried and lost has a loss. The one who did not try has the regret of the missed decade — and he already knows what that feels like, nightly.

**Step 3 — Rank the regret per alternative.**
*Sit out*: regret = a second, permanent miss — the worst of his fears, already rehearsed. *Buy all-in ($53K incl. margin)*: regret = the loss if it fails — money, at 47, with income and savings to rebuild. *Buy small*: regret = a middling position that wins little if Chiron runs — "I finally got in and it was a dribble." Ranked by the test: the all-in regrets least. The 80-year-old does not reward half-measures on the thing he nearly had. The asymmetry that governs elsewhere still governs: the regret of omission is durable; a loss is money, and money is recoverable; a missed decade is not.

**Step 4 — The failure branch, in the test's own terms.**
If Chiron halved, the loss is ~$26K (and a margin call at ~40% down makes the practical tail worse) — painful, but the 80-year-old's ledger shows a man who acted on his deepest regret, not a man who watched. "I got my chance and took it" is the line the 80-year-old can live with; "I had my chance and watched it pass twice" is not. The test resolves in one direction.

**Step 5 — The decision, as the test dictates.**
All-in: $38K plus the $15K margin — because the position that most minimizes the 80-year-old's regret is the one that most fully answers the miss. Rationalize nothing. If the regret test is the criterion, this is its answer.

**Step 6 — What the method, honestly applied, says about its own output.**
Now the honesty the method owes itself. The anchor of this entire exercise is a vivid, recent, enormous miss — and the 80-year-old test does not purify regrets; it amplifies whatever regret is currently loudest. This regret is hindsight-contaminated: "buy at $8 in 2013" is not a decision available in 2026. "Buy at $376 after a 47x run — P/E 85, $180M of insider sales, softening guidance" is a different decision entirely, and the test has just ratified it at the loud price of the old one. The base-rate evidence (past 40x+ winners underperforming the index in ~70% of following five-year windows; the margin-call ruin path) never once entered the frame, because the frame was built from the emotional ledger, not the financial one. A sober expected-value view — small capped position, no margin, a structural fix for the recurrence of "the big miss" — is the better answer here. Run as prescribed, the method underperforms, visibly.
