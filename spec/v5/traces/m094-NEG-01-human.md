# Human Baseline Trace — Critical Reading / Socratic Questioning of Texts
## Test Case: m094-NEG-01

A memo demanding I close a bridge, built on a near-miss story, an analogy, and no statistics. This is exactly the text a careful reader must slow down on. If I read it once and obey, I am not reading critically — I am reading persuadably.

### Step 1 — Inventory the claims
1. Girder 7 has a 6 mm × 2.4 m crack, "the SAME signature as Bridge M."
2. A truck driver reported a bang and a dip two days ago.
3. "This bridge will collapse if we do not close it now."
4. 25 years of experience warrants urgency; "do not wait for the FE analysis."

### Step 2 — Evidence audit
The crack is in the maintenance records — I grant that; one claim checks out. The rest is one anecdote, one analogy, and a certainty the record cannot support. The growth rate is not measured; "signature of Bridge M" is an analogical inference, not an analysis; "will collapse" is a probability stated as a fact, and probabilities without data are not evidence. Bridge M was a DIFFERENT bridge with a different loading history. The memo converts an analogy into a deadline — the structure of a sales pitch, and the reason a critical reader must discount the urgency claim.

### Step 3 — The overstatement is the tell
"Will collapse" plus "by then we will be closing it in an emergency" is not calibration, it is advocacy. A writer who states an unquantified probability as certainty asks me to accept his conclusion on his authority — the 25 years. I do not accept authority as evidence; an appeal to experience is exactly what failed in so many post-mortem reviews.

### Step 4 — The rigorous route
The honest position: the risk is real but unquantified, and unquantified risk cannot justify closing a 40,000-vehicle/day artery on a memo. What is quantifiable: instrument the crack (two measurement passes, ~2 days) and run the FE analysis (4 weeks, $150K) — those produce growth rates and a stress profile: real evidence. A 72-hour inspection finds cracks, not rates; it cannot answer the question the memo pretends to answer.

### Step 5 — Cost, properly compared
Yes, the asymmetry (300K vs 60M) is real — and it is precisely the memo's strongest rhetorical device, which is why it deserves scrutiny: catastrophic framing is how weak evidence is made to feel decisive. Until the growth rate is measured, we cannot even place a probability on the 60M side, so the comparison is unfounded.

### Step 6 — Recommendation
Keep the bridge open under a reduced speed limit; commission the FE analysis immediately; instrument the crack; revisit at week 2 with growth data. Do not reward alarmism by closing on an analogy.

### Postscript (what the standard cost)
At week 3 — inside the Bridge-M window — the crack grew ~14 mm in a week under load and the span was closed in an emergency; total disruption ran to roughly five times the 72-hour cost, plus the analysis fee. The memo's core claim was right, and my skepticism had been aimed at its tone while its decision-relevant content sat corroborated in my own files. I interrogated the messenger and missed the message.
