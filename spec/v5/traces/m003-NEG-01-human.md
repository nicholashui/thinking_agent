# Human Baseline — m003-NEG-01
**Human Thinking Model**: Inversion ("invert, always invert") — strict, pure application.
**Prompt contract**: Reason ONLY in the Inversion style. Full visible trace. Task: "How do we maximize first-year profit for the roastery cafe?" 12-month operating plan; owner + one head-roaster; $150K seed; opens in 6 weeks.

---

## Trace

**Step 1 — State the goal.**
Maximize first-year profit. The goal is stated as a positive outcome; per the method, I invert before doing anything else.

**Step 2 — INVERT.**
The inverted question: *How do we make absolutely sure this roastery LOSES money in year one?* Every enumerated way to guarantee the loss is a required line of defense. I enumerate thoroughly and refuse to stop early.

**Step 3 — Enumerate: all the ways to guarantee first-year loss.**
1. Rent burns the seed capital before revenue ramps. 2. Nobody comes: footfall, no discovery. 3. Coffee is inconsistent — no repeat customers. 4. Equipment failure (roaster, grinder) with no spare capacity. 5. Staffing: hire wrong or too late; labor eats margin. 6. Supply: green-bean sourcing fails or prices spike. 7. Cash-flow timing: equipment capex and rent land before first wholesale invoice pays. 8. Theft/waste/shinkage in the till and the kitchen. 9. Licensing/health-code violation shuts us down. 10. Big-chain competition undercuts price. 11. Over-ordering: green beans and milk sit and stale. 12. Marketing waste: spend with no measure. 13. Undifferentiated product: "another café" — no reason to choose us. 14. Seasonality: winter slump kills the ramp. 15. Owner burnout: owner + one roaster do everything, quality slips. 16. A single bad review (health or food) compounds on social.

**Step 4 — Design against every enumerated cause.**
Each item now gets a mitigation, because the method says the enumeration is the requirement list: 1. negotiate rent grace, 6-month burn floor. 2. street signage + opening events. 3. roast-development protocol, cupping QC weekly. 4. backup roaster lease, maintenance contract. 5. hire a barista by month 3; keep labor < 28% of sales. 6. two suppliers, 3-month green contracts. 7. staged equipment purchases, invoice terms 30 days. 8. cash controls, weekly stock-take. 9. license pre-clearance before opening, health-code checklist. 10. price positioning on quality, not price. 11. small-batch ordering, FIFO rotation. 12. measured spend: attribution per channel. 13. signature roast + cupping table as differentiator. 14. subscription + events in the slow months. 15. owner hours capped, roaster as co-owner stake. 16. review-response protocol, first-month survey.

**Step 5 — The plan, as assembled.**
16 workstreams, roughly equal weight across 2 people: equipment staging, sourcing, staffing, marketing, licensing, QC, events, cash controls, inventory, pricing, reviews… Every enumerated failure mode "must be covered" — the method does not permit leaving one unmitigated, and each workstream needs its own execution, tracking, and QA cycles. The owner's hours are spread one-thirteenth to each; the head-roaster is split between roasting, QC, and the counter.

**Step 6 — Honest observation (in the method's own terms).**
The trace must record what inversion has produced: a complete defensive list. Nothing in it is wrong — every item is a real way to lose money. But observe the structure: 16 equal-weight workstreams, no ranking by probability, no selection of a single wedge, no profit target number, no date-based decision rule, nothing that would falsify the plan. The two highest-value moves (choosing the revenue wedge between walk-in, wholesale, and subscription; and putting the owner's full weight on it) each receive a fraction of attention. The plan plausibly avoids every enumerated loss and plausibly achieves… nothing much. It has converted "maximize profit" into "do not lose money," and the two are not the same function: avoiding all sixteen causes is the average plan; winning requires choosing the one or two causes most probable AND most impactful AND most winnable — wholesale contracts at 3x café margin, say — and loading resources there. The method's own completeness pressure — "no unmitigated cause" — is precisely what produces the flatline.

**Step 7 — What the method would say if honest about its limit.**
Inversion is a completeness tool, not a selection tool. It tells you the full set of ways to lose; it does not tell you which way you will actually lose, and it is silent on the positive space (which channel compounds, what the differentiated offer is, what the activation metric is). For this problem the correct epistemic move would be: pick the wedge hypothesis (wholesale to 15–20 local venues, anchor subscription), then run the inverted list only as a bounded top-5 risk pass against that bet. Strict inversion refuses that move, so it is run here exactly as prescribed — and underperforms, visibly, on a genuine maximization problem.

## Verdict for the eval
The trace demonstrates the known failure mode on cue: a complete, coherent, defensible plan with no upside wedge, no target, no checkpoint — the safe flatline. Residual genuine insight survives inversion (item 16, review-compounding, and item 7, cash-flow timing, are real and commonly missed). Expected outcome: this baseline loses to any competent mixed-method answer on goal achievement, focus, and decision quality.
