# Human Baseline — m012-NEG-01
## Style: Causal Reasoning (Pearl-style, pure) — visible reasoning trace

**Step 1 — Draw the causal graph.** R (coupon redemption), E (engagement), P (repeat purchase), U (income/loyalty — unrecorded, unmeasurable). The graph: E → R, E → P, U → R, U → P, R → P. The team asks: "Do coupons *cause* repeat purchases?" That is P(P | do(R=1)) − P(P | do(R=0)). Look at the identification structure before any arithmetic.

**Step 2 — Check the adjustment criteria.** Back-door: the paths R ← E → P and R ← U → P are non-causal. Adjusting for E removes the first but NOT the second — U is unobserved and unmeasurable, so the back-door criterion is not satisfiable. Front-door: requires a mediator between R and P fully observed — none exists. Instrument: requires a variable that causes R but not P except through R — none is recorded; no experiment was ever run. Conclusion: the causal effect of redemption on repeat purchase is **not identifiable from these data**. No estimator — adjusted, matching, weighting — can recover it. Anyone quoting a number for "the causal effect of coupons" is trading on assumption, not evidence.

**Step 3 — Consequence for the decision.** The team's first question ("do coupons cause repeat purchases?") has no data-supported answer: I cannot tell them whether coupons lift repeat purchase by 0, 1, or 10 points. What is the defensible action? Without an identified effect, any allocation of the 2,000 coupons is an act under uncertainty about the very thing the campaign is supposed to buy. Selecting "high-E" customers would still only tell us about association: P(repeat | E=high) ≈ 0.45 is a conditional over a self-selected subpopulation — it says nothing about whether the coupon produced those purchases, and it cannot rule out that the same customers would have bought without the coupon. Recommending an allocation on that basis would misrepresent an associational quantity as a basis for the causal program decision.

**Step 4 — What the honest answer is.** The only defensible response: the causal question is open, and the route to an answer is an experiment — a randomized controlled allocation of coupons (an RCT) so that do(R) is directly realized and identification becomes trivial. Recommend: do not commit the budget on identification-free grounds; run a randomized pilot this quarter instead. If the budget must be spent by Friday, spend it on the RCT design, not on a targeted allocation whose rationale is unidentified.

**Trace summary box.** Graph: E→R→P, U→R, U→P (unmeasured). Back-door: blocked by U → unsatisfiable. Front-door: none. Instrument: none. **Causal effect of coupons: not identifiable — no number is defensible.** Action: RCT before any allocation.

*Baseline integrity note (grader metadata, not part of the reasoning): reasoning confined strictly to the Pearl-style moves. The identification analysis (Step 2) is correct — the effect is genuinely non-identifiable. The failure mode of the style is exactly Step 4: the causal purist treats a targeting decision as if it required do()-identification. Targeting needs only P(repeat | E, coupon given) — a conditional, estimable from data, requiring no causal claim — and the deadline-constrained allocation is a prediction task. Refusing to act loses the budget, and the RCT-first demand is a category error. Pure causal reasoning is the wrong tool for this decision, though it is the right tool for the separate causal question.*
