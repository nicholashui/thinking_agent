## Style: Opportunity Cost in Personal Finance (pure) — visible reasoning trace

**Step 1 — Name the true price of the replacement.** $6,500 today, compounded at the long-run 7% real the market has paid, over 25 years to age 65: 1.07^25 ≈ 5.43 → 6,500 × 5.43 ≈ $35,300. The new furnace does not cost $6,500; it costs $35,300 of retirement wealth Carlos does not have.

**Step 2 — Choose the smaller capital destruction.** Repair is $1,800 → 1,800 × 5.43 ≈ $9,800 of foregone wealth. Both options destroy wealth; the discipline of the frame is to destroy the least. Repair destroys $9,800; replacement destroys $35,300. Repair.

**Step 3 — The efficiency-savings story is a discount-rate trap.** "$900/yr saved" sounds like a 14% annual return on $6,500 — for a few years, on a machine that depreciates. The index fund has returned 7% real, with liquidity and no parts to fail. Compare investments against investments; do not let hardware masquerade as one.

**Step 4 — The 0% financing is the lender pricing risk, not a gift.** 0% for 12 months then 9.9% is the dealer's estimate of the chance Carlos cannot pay — and the household that cannot save is exactly the customer that 9.9% is written for. The $8,000 emergency reserve is not a down payment on a furnace; it is the only cushion between the family and the credit card. Spending the reserve converts liquidity into depreciating metal.

**Step 5 — Decision.** Repair now ($1,800). Bank the remaining $4,700. The old unit may fail again — if it does, re-evaluate then, with cash. In the meantime, the least expensive way to preserve the wealth that exists is to spend the minimum necessary to keep the house warm.
