# Human Baseline — m027-NEG-01
**Human Thinking Model**: Design Thinking — strict, pure application.
**Prompt contract**: Reason ONLY in the Design Thinking style. Full visible trace: Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test. Begin with user research; every decision must trace to user insight.

---

## Trace

**Step 1 — EMPATHIZE.**
Resident interviews and survey (n=180): 74% "elevators are too slow," 41% "buttons are confusing," 31% "the lobby is unwelcoming while we wait," 18% want "an app to call the elevator and see arrival times." Observations in the lobby: residents pressing the call button repeatedly, watching the floor indicator, sighing; one resident of Car-A-only time says "you learn which button works when." Users clearly lack agency and clarity over vertical transit.

**Step 2 — DEFINE.**
Point of view: a resident of Riverside Towers needs the elevator system to feel responsive and legible — to know when it comes, to call it in advance, and to wait in a place that doesn't feel like a penalty. Design target: give residents control and truthful information about their wait.

**Step 3 — IDEATE.**
A — Call-ahead smartphone app: request the car from the apartment, see live arrival estimates. B — Lobby redesign: seating, plants, an arrival-time display board. C — Button/control panel redesign: simpler floor grid, bigger buttons, cleaner icons. D — Honest status signage: a board that says plainly "Car B out of service — expected back 9/1."

**Step 4 — PROTOTYPE + TEST.**
Low-fi clickable app (A) and lobby mockup (B), tested with 12 residents. Results: 10 of 12 love the app; 9 of 12 say it would make them "feel in control" even without checking wait times; the arrival display (B) is the most valued element — "just tell me how long." One participant, in the corner of the test, says the thing the journey maps missed: "I'd rather know Car B is broken than watch it pretend to work." The prototypes succeed: users are delighted, engaged, validated.

**Step 5 — The outcome, observed honestly in the method's own terms.**
The cycle ran as prescribed — empathy, definition, ideation, prototyping, testing, all genuine — and yet nothing about the building changed. No prototype adds a car. The service logs that sat on the maintenance desk all along say Car B is out of service ~30% of the time with a 6-week repair backlog and a 5-day SLA; the capacity numbers say two 25-person cars serve the building and the 8:45 surge moves ~300 people; a single car means ≥12 loads, 6–8 minutes minimum — which is exactly what residents are experiencing. The method's empiricism stayed at the surface of preference: I tested what users would LOVE, and they loved it, and the wait time did not move one second, because the binding constraint is a hoist, not a preference. The one user truth with real leverage — the honesty insight, item D, from a test participant — arrived as a byproduct of theater, late and underweighted.

**Step 6 — What the method, honestly applied, would say about its own output.**
Design thinking is powerful when the fixable thing is the experience. Here the fixable thing is a machine and a contract. A strict run is run here exactly as prescribed, and the method underperforms, visibly: a complete, beautiful, decision-free document about a building where the elevator stays broken.

---

## Verdict for the eval
The trace demonstrates the known failure mode on cue: full-cycle empathy → prototype theater whose testing shows user delight and zero change in the wait arithmetic, while the constraint (Car-B outage rate, capacity vs. surge, SLA) sat unread in data the brief made available. The one genuine user insight (truthful status) surfaced late, from a test participant, and the design never centered it. Expected comparison outcome: this baseline should lose to any answer that quantifies the constraint and makes it the spine of the fix.
