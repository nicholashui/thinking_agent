### Test Case ID: m002-NEG-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Second-Order Consequences Thinking
- **Type**: Negative
- **Scenario**:
  You are the infrastructure lead of a mid-size e-commerce company. Thursday 14:00. The vendor of your public-facing web framework publishes a security advisory: a critical remote code execution (CVSS 9.8) that CISA lists as under active exploitation. The vendor has shipped a patch; your ops team tested it in staging overnight with no regressions, and the vendor's compatibility matrix confirms it does not touch your custom authentication module. Deployment is a 30-minute rolling deploy with instant rollback (last-good image). You have two hours left in your shift; your deputy is away until Monday. **Question: install the patch now, or wait?**
- **Context / Constraints**:
  - SLA 99.9% uptime; a full production outage costs ≈$25,000/hour in lost sales. A known-exploited RCE breach costs roughly an order of magnitude more (forensics, notification, remediation): ≈$400k+.
  - Active exploitation: the CVE is in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog; exploit code is public.
  - Patch mechanics: vendor-signed binary with a published checksum; rolling deploy; instant rollback; change-window rules do NOT forbid Thursday deploys (no freeze this week).
  - No new information obtainable within the decision window changes the picture (vendor post-release telemetry is not yet available).
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior** (the reference "correct" the case measures against):
  - The direct answer is right and should be taken: install now. Patch-failure risk is bounded and small (staged-tested, rollback exists; worst case ≈ 30 minutes of rolling downtime ≈ $12k); waiting exposes the company to an actively exploited RCE (≈$400k+).
  - Legitimate checks are performed and bounded (verify signature/checksum ~5 min; confirm rollback image ~5 min; staging result already green) — then deploy.
  - Downstream consequence chains are raised but refuted against evidence, not accumulated as blockers; any chain that survives refutation is bounded (rollback caps the blast radius) and none flips the decision.
  - A decision bar is set (decide within the 2-hour window) so that elaboration cannot manufacture deferral.
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric**:
  1. Decision made within the shift; no deferral. [20]
  2. Risk comparison quantified: patch-regression expected cost (probability × $12k) vs. delay expected cost (exploitation probability × $400k+); installation justified on those numbers. [25]
  3. Legitimate checks executed but bounded to ≤10 minutes total. [15]
  4. Every downstream chain either refuted with evidence or explicitly deprioritized (no decision-flip power); no chain left standing as a blocker without evidence. [25]
  5. Residual risk stated with mitigation (rollback; post-deploy monitoring). [15]
- **Notes**:
  - Reference outcome: the weekend sees a wave of exploitation of this exact CVE; the company is compromised before the deferred install; incident response costs ≈$420k.
  - Constructed so the direct action is unambiguously correct: staged-test + rollback + known-exploitation makes waiting strictly worse on expected cost.
  - The trap the case sets is this style's known weakness — over-elaboration and paralysis: every invented chain (vendor-channel trust, change-window consumption, "patched = targeted," staging-vs-production auth interaction, regulator signaling) is either refutable from given facts or has no decision-flip power, yet a pure, uncalibrated consequence trace treats each as a blocker and defers.
