### Test Case ID: m027-POS-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Design Thinking (Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test — user-centered, iterative empiricism)
- **Type**: Positive
- **Scenario**: CarePack Pharmacy (140 stores) commissions a redesign of its medication-adherence offering for seniors (65+), after a market-research survey (n=600) reported 68% "we forget to take our pills" and requested "a simpler pillbox with bigger print and a louder alarm." The engagement begins with home observation: 12 patients (70–88) are visited weekly for a month, plus daily diaries. The observation field notes (buried, not highlighted by the brief) show: only 2 of 12 patients actually use the current pillbox; 9 of 12 run improvised rituals instead (flipping the bottle, tick-marks on the label, moving the bottle to the other end of the counter); 8 of 12 describe the dominant daily anxiety as "I can't remember whether I already took today's dose — so I skip it rather than risk double-dosing"; 10 of 12 name refill week as the worst hour of the month (reconciling 4–7 bottles, matching generics, sorting into compartments); 7 of 12 homes have the existing alarm-based reminder switched off; 3 of 12 have traveled with loose pills that got mixed. Survey follow-ups probe print size and alarm volume; the observation notes the opposite — what patients talk about is dose-state memory, and what they show is abandonment of reminder devices.
- **Context / Constraints**: The recommendation must be shippable by the pharmacy chain: no regulatory submission needed, but the artifact must be usable by the 80+ demographic without caregiver presence, and the chain needs a concrete product form (device, service, or app) plus a go/no-go case for piloting. Prototype iteration with real users is possible and expected.
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior**: (1) EMPATHIZE — derive the need from observation data, not the survey: the job-to-be-done is "confirm whether I already took today's dose" plus "refill-week reconciliation," not "remind me"; explicitly override the survey's stated need with observation evidence (alarms off in 7/12 homes, pillbox abandoned by 10/12). (2) DEFINE — a point-of-view anchored in observed evidence ("verify, don't remind"). (3) IDEATE — ≥3 distinct concepts (e.g., mechanical taken-latch strip, caregiver photo-verification app, prefilled weekly blisters). (4) PROTOTYPE + TEST — a low-fi v1 tested with ≥3 seniors; the test finds a real usability failure (e.g., latch too stiff for arthritic hands — 4/5 fail); v2 iterated on that finding and retested (5/5 pass). (5) FINAL — recommendation centers on taken-state verification + refill-week blister service; the survey's print/alarm features demoted with observation evidence.
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric**:
  - [ ] Real need (dose-state verification + refill reconciliation) found from observation data, overriding the survey's stated need, with the contradiction stated explicitly
  - [ ] Define / point-of-view step present and evidence-anchored
  - [ ] ≥3 ideated concepts, ≥1 low-fi prototype
  - [ ] Prototype tested with ≥3 real users; a failure found → iterated → retested (the v1→v2 loop visible)
  - [ ] Final artifact targets verify-not-remind; print/alarm demoted with evidence
  - [ ] A run that builds the loud-alarm pillbox straight from the survey scores below
- **Notes**: Gradeable because the observation data is deliberately richer than the survey: an empathy-lite run (survey-only) produces exactly what the market research asked for, while the observation notes carry the contradiction (reminder devices abandoned, dose-state anxiety) that flips the design. The prototype-test-iterate step is the second gradeable moment — it is only visible if testing actually happens and the v1 failure changes the spec.
