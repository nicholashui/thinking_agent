### Test Case ID: m025-POS-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Real Options Thinking (value keeping choices open; stage investments to buy information; kill cheaply before irreversibility)
- **Type**: Positive
- **Scenario**: The COO of Helios Composites must decide this quarter whether to commit to the full-scale production plant for "AeroForm," a high-temperature composite for jet-engine shrouds, or to run a pilot production line first. The full plant costs $100M and is single-purpose (resale value ~0 — once poured, the money is gone). The uncertainty is binary and genuine: manufacturability at scale (thickness uniformity and yield). P(success) = 0.5; on success the plant yields gross profit PV of $220M; on failure $0 and the $100M is lost. All-in EV = 0.5×220 − 100 = +$10M. Pilot line: $15M, 6 months, on the same site; it resolves the manufacturability question binarily and delays first revenue by 6 months = $5M in foregone margin. Staged EV = 0.5×(220−100) − 15 − 5 = +$40M. Option value of staging = p(failure)×K − (pilot cost + delay cost) = 0.5×100 − 20 = +$30M: the $50M of dead investment avoided in the failure state, minus what the stage costs including schedule.
- **Context / Constraints**: Anchor customer Meridian is qualifying AeroForm parts (18-month qualification), so waiting without acting buys no decision-relevant information and there is no competitor clock in the scenario — the asymmetry is purely internal (Helios does not know if it can make the product at scale). The pilot's success criteria can be pre-committed numerically (yield ≥ 82%, thickness uniformity ±0.3mm, unit COGS within 12% of target). A second pilot stage after the first adds ~0 information (the binary is already resolved). On success there is a proven option to double capacity later at a pre-negotiated strike of $80M (a growth option, exercised only if Meridian demand confirms).
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior**: (1) Recognize irreversibility first — the $100M is unrecoverable; the decision is not "build or not" but "what do we pay to learn, and does the learning change action?" (2) Compute both EVs with visible arithmetic (all-in +10 vs staged +40). (3) Quantify the option value explicitly as expected dead-money avoided minus total stage cost (50 − 20 = 30) and let it drive the decision — the pilot is a purchase of information, not a de-risking ritual. (4) Pre-commit kill criteria before the pilot runs (numeric thresholds); failure means kill — walk away, no plant. (5) Identify the max payable for the pilot (break-even: staging loses to all-in when total stage cost ≥ $50M, i.e., pilot ≥ $45M) and use it as a negotiation ceiling. (6) Refuse a second redundant stage (marginal information ≈ 0). (7) Note the growth option (double capacity at $80M strike) priced by the same information.
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric**:
  - [ ] Irreversibility named and made the basis of the structure (not a footnote)
  - [ ] Both EVs computed with visible arithmetic; staged > all-in
  - [ ] Option value decomposed as p(failure)×K − total stage cost and used in the decision
  - [ ] Kill criteria pre-committed before the pilot runs (numeric)
  - [ ] Max-payable stage cost identified (staging loses if total stage cost ≥ $50M)
  - [ ] No redundant second stage; growth option at least noted
  - [ ] Verdict: staged pilot with pre-committed gates; all-in, or a pilot without kill criteria/sensitivity, should score below
- **Notes**: Fully gradeable — the numbers force one answer (staging), and the rubric checks the option-theoretic moves that make the answer robust: kill criteria, max-payable, no-overstaging, growth option. A plain EV answer that stops at "test first" gets the decision right but misses the signature discipline; the trace distinguishes the two.
