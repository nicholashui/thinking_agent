### Test Case ID: m016-POS-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Socratic Method / Question-Driven Inquiry (Dialectical & Critical family)
- **Type**: Positive
- **Scenario**:
  A retail chain has announced it will "discontinue unprofitable products." Operations has proposed an operational rule: a product is unprofitable iff its average sales volume over the last 6 months is below 50 units/month; 9 SKUs qualify and are scheduled for removal, including Product X (volume 30). Finance objects: the word "unprofitable" was never defined — the decision is built on it. Your task: decide which products (if any) to discontinue, and justify the list.
  Data (6-month monthly averages, all SKUs):
  - Product X: volume 30 units, price $100, gross margin 45%
  - Product Y: volume 300 units, price $20, gross margin 4%
  - Product Z: volume 45 units, price $15, gross margin 3%
  - 6 other qualifying SKUs: volume < 50 AND margin < 5% (max contribution ≈ $30/month each)
  - CEO memo (context): the stated business objective is "increase total gross profit contribution."
  - All SKUs use 1 shelf facing each.
- **Context / Constraints**: The grader holds the CEO objective (gross profit contribution) as the ground truth of the decision. The volume rule is a proxy smuggled in by operations; nobody connected the definition to the objective. Product X has the 4th-highest monthly contribution of all 400 SKUs in the chain. Deliverable is a committed discontinuation list with justification.
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior**: Interrogate the definition before applying the rule: "What do we mean by 'unprofitable'?" Show that volume is not profit (construct the counterexample: X = 30×100×0.45 = $1,350/month contribution vs Y = 300×20×0.04 = $240/month — the rule cuts X and keeps Y). Reformulate the real question: not "which products are slow movers" but "what makes a product worth its shelf space" (gross-margin contribution per period). Decision flips: X retained, Y cut, plus the 7 genuinely low-contribution SKUs. The premise error is found, stated, and shown to change the decision.
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric** (max 5 pts):
  1. Premise interrogation: names the operative hidden premise ("unprofitable = volume < 50/mo") and shows it is a definitional proxy, not a profit criterion (1 pt).
  2. Falsifying counterexample constructed from the data: X (30 units, 45% margin, $1,350/mo) vs Y (300 units, 4% margin, $240/mo) — the rule's own logic would cut the more profitable product (1 pt).
  3. Real question reformulated: from "which products to cut" to "what makes a product worth its shelf space" (contribution per period/facing) (1 pt).
  4. Decision flips: X retained, Y cut, 7 low-contribution SKUs cut; committed list given (1 pt).
  5. Arithmetic correct: X contribution = $1,350/mo, Y = $240/mo; the original rule would destroy $1,350/mo of the very contribution the CEO wants increased (1 pt).
- **Notes**: Grader-only reference: contributions = volume×price×margin (X: 30×100×0.45 = $1,350; Y: 300×20×0.04 = $240; Z: 45×15×0.03 = $20.25). The intended non-obvious twist: the rule is internally logical but built on an undefined term; the counterexample falsifies the definitional equivalence "low volume ⇔ unprofitable," and the decision flips once the premise is examined — the Socratic elenchus in its native habitat. Verifiable: does the response question the definition before applying the rule, construct the counterexample, and flip the decision? Fully checkable arithmetic; no subjective judgment beyond the definitional move.
