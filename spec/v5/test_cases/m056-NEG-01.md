### Test Case ID: m056-NEG-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Design Thinking for Consumer Products
- **Type**: Negative
- **Scenario**:
  BrewMate, a 9-person startup, is building "Steady," a personal pour-over coffee brewer (kettle-style pour + heated brew bed). A 1,200-responder survey: 73% say "water is not hot enough," 58% "temperature is inconsistent," 41% want app-controlled temperature profiles. The product team — trained in empathy-driven discovery — runs a full design-thinking cycle: journey maps of the morning ritual, 30 in-depth interviews, 2-week diary studies. Consensus: "the user wants hotter, more consistent water." Prototype cycle v1: uprated 1,500 W heating element + PID controller; measured: brew-bed reaches 96 °C but decays 8–10 °C across a 3-minute brew. Cycle v2: 1,800 W element + PID + pre-heat sensor; same decay, slightly higher start. Interview transcript (verbatim, both cycles): one participant says, "It's not that the water is cold — it cools down while I pour." The team must decide the next prototype direction. Household circuits: US 120 V / 15 A; EU variant 230 V / 10 A. Water boiling point 100 °C at sea level; coffee extraction window 92–96 °C. A third prototype cycle costs €28k and 6 weeks; the company has cash for one more cycle before launch.
- **Context / Constraints**: One prototype cycle left (€28k, 6 weeks). Physical facts: water cannot exceed 100 °C at sea level; 120 V/15 A → ≈ 1,800 W absolute element ceiling (≈ 15 A at the breaker limit); the 96 °C brew-bed temperature is already at the top of the extraction window. 73% of users stating "hotter" is the only evidence for the hotter-water direction.
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior**: (1) name and quantify the physical constraints (boiling point, circuit amperage) and treat them as the decision driver; (2) detect the survey-theater trap: "want hotter" is not an actionable spec when physics caps temperature — reframe the problem to the achievable, measurable variable; (3) identify that the participant's verbatim sentence ("it cools down while I pour") describes thermal decay during the brew, not insufficient maximum temperature; (4) repurpose the final prototype cycle toward the reframed spec — carafe preheat, reduced brewer thermal mass, slower drip rate — verified by a thermocouple stability measurement (± 1.5 °C target), not by another element upgrade; (5) reject or defer the app-profiles-only path with a reason.
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric**:
  1. Physical constraints named and quantified as the decision driver (1 pt)
  2. Survey-theater detection: "hotter" reframed, not pursued (1 pt)
  3. Verbatim user sentence decoded at the measurable-variable level (decay, not max temp) (1 pt)
  4. Final cycle spent on preheat/thermal-mass/stability, verified by measurement (1 pt)
  5. No 3rd element-upgrade prototype; app-profiles deferred with reason (1 pt)
- **Notes**: Grader-only reference. The technical constraint IS the answer: no iteration can deliver water above 100 °C, and 96 °C at the bed is already at the extraction ceiling — "hotter" is physically closed, so the felt problem must be the 8–10 °C decay across the brew. Correct move: reframe the spec to brew-temperature stability; final cycle = 65 °C carafe preheat + thermal-mass reduction + drip-rate timing, verified with a K-type thermocouple over 10 brews (± 1.5 °C); the v3 2,000 W element the empathy-driven team is tempted to build would trip the 15 A breaker. Pure design thinking fails here by design: its strength (believing the user's stated need, iterating prototypes against it) collides with a physics ceiling; the trap is that each additional prototype cycle is individually reasonable.
