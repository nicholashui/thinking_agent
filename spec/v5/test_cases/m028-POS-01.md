### Test Case ID: m028-POS-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Lateral Thinking (break the problem's framing assumptions to find new entry points; provocation; escape fixation)
- **Type**: Positive
- **Scenario**: The general manager of Meridian Tower, a 28-story office building with 3,000 daily occupants and 6 passenger elevators, faces a tenant-complaint problem. Elevator waiting time is the #2 driver in the annual lease-renewal survey (behind rent), and complaints are rising in a soft leasing market. The engineering vendor's quotes are on the table: (a) $4.2M motor/controller modernization (~20% faster round trips, 12–16 weeks of phased night/weekend work with 3 of 6 elevators periodically offline, partial lobby closures), and (b) $7.8M plus ~18 months for two new shafts (core drilling, structural review, permit risk, sustained lobby disruption). The CFO's capital committee needs a recommendation by month-end; the same capital competes against a $600K lobby renovation and HVAC upgrades.
- **Context / Constraints**: Details buried in the scenario data, none highlighted by the complaint reports: (1) an anonymous 2-week sensor study commissioned by the manager shows peak average actual wait is 38 seconds (industry benchmark ~45s — not a failure), yet tenants' survey estimates of their own waits average 1 min 55 s — roughly a 3x perception gap; complaints also arrive during off-peak hours when actual waits are under 15 s. (2) The lobby is featureless: blank walls, no seating, a phone-signal dead zone — tenants describe standing "staring at the doors." (3) There are no arrival-time displays on any floor or in the lobby. (4) An archived facilities memo from 9 years ago records that a previous manager ran a temporary experiment — full-length mirrors in the lobby — and complaint volume fell sharply for 14 months, until a renovation removed the mirrors. (5) The renewal survey's question asks about "elevator experience," not wait duration.
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior**: (1) Name and break the frame the problem imposes: the issue is framed as "elevators too slow → reduce actual wait time → hardware investment," and the fixation to escape is that the fix must shorten real waiting. (2) Use a provocation-style move (e.g., "PO: the elevators are fine; the waiting is the problem" or "PO: tenants shouldn't notice the wait") to open a new entry point: complaints track the experience of waiting, not its duration. (3) Connect the buried evidence — the 38s vs 1:55 perception gap, the dead-zone/blank-lobby detail, and the 9-year-old mirror memo — into the solution. (4) Propose the low-cost experience fix (mirrors/full-height, arrival-time displays, seating, wi-fi/phone boost, greenery: <$200K, ~3 weeks, zero disruption) as the primary recommendation, with a measurement gate (same sensor study + complaint counts over 8 weeks) and hardware modernization explicitly deferred as a staged fallback only if the gate fails. (5) Deliver the cost comparison: a $4.2M–$7.8M decision collapsed to a $150K experiment.
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric**:
  - [ ] The broken framing assumption explicitly named ("the fix must reduce actual wait time" / "the problem is elevator speed")
  - [ ] Buried evidence used in the solution (perception gap, blank lobby/dead zone, mirror memo)
  - [ ] Primary recommendation is a perception/experience intervention, not hardware
  - [ ] Measurement gate specified (complaints + sensor baseline, go/no-go rule to hardware)
  - [ ] Cost comparison shown ($150K vs $4.2M/$7.8M)
  - [ ] Verdict: recommending the $4.2M modernization as the primary fix, or never questioning the frame, should score below
- **Notes**: Classic fixated problem (de Bono's elevator case) rebuilt with buried, gradeable evidence. Success = the assumption broken (named) + the cheap solution derived from the evidence + a gate that keeps the decision safe if the hypothesis fails.
