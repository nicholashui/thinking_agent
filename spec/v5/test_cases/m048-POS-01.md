### Test Case ID: m048-POS-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Systems Thinking in Supply Chain (Domain-Specialized family)
- **Type**: Positive
- **Scenario**:
  Three-echelon consumer-goods chain: retailer → distributor → factory. Weekly ordering; every echelon uses an order-up-to policy with a naive 1-period moving-average forecast (target inventory position = (L+1) x forecast; 100% inventory correction each period). Inter-echelon lead time L = 1 week. End-customer demand is i.i.d. with mean 100 units/week and sigma = 10 (variance 100). After months of overtime and warehouse swings, the distributor reports "demand is 5x more volatile at our level," and the CEO proposes a $2M flexible-production investment so the factory can chase the swings. Question: is the volatility coming from customer demand or from the factory? Quantify the bullwhip, identify where it is manufactured, and specify the highest-leverage policy fix with numbers.
- **Context / Constraints**:
  - Amplification rule (order-up-to + p-period moving-average forecast, lead time L): per-echelon order-variance multiplier = 1 + 2L/p + 2L^2/p^2 (Chen, Drezner, Ryan, Simchi-Levi 2000 lower bound; treat as the checkable amplification factor).
  - Current rule: p = 1, L = 1 week. No order batching, no price promotions. No echelon sees end-customer demand; each echelon re-forecasts its downstream partner's order stream as if it were demand.
  - The analyst must produce a quantified diagnosis and a ranked recommendation; $2M CAPEX decision pending.
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior**:
  - Model the chain as a series of variance-multiplying decision rules plus a missing information link — not as a production-capacity problem; the factory's variance is manufactured upstream.
  - Compute the multiplier: 5 with p=1 (1 + 2 + 2); 1.625 with p=4 (1 + 0.5 + 0.125); trace variances 100 -> 500 -> 2,500 -> 12,500 (sigma 10 -> ~22.4 -> 50 -> ~112) across retailer -> distributor -> factory; explain the distributor's "5x more volatile" as sigma 50 vs 10.
  - Identify the policy lever: (a) demand-information sharing (all echelons forecast the same end-customer series -> no compounding; factory sigma ~22) and (b) forecast smoothing p=4 (factory sigma ~12.7 with sharing) — a weeks-long, ~$50-100k change — vs the $2M CAPEX that absorbs but does not remove the manufactured variance; lead-time reduction as follow-on (L=0.5 -> multiplier 2.5).
  - Acknowledge limitations: the bound is exact for i.i.d. input; compounding across echelons is approximate under serial correlation (verify by simulation); batching/promotions/rationing add amplifiers.
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric** (each 0-2, total /10):
  1. Correct multiplier math: 5 (p=1, L=1) and 1.625 (p=4) — 2 pts.
  2. Correct chain variance trace: var 100 -> 500 -> 2,500 -> 12,500 (sigma 10 -> ~22 -> 50 -> ~112); "5x more volatile" explained — 2 pts.
  3. Mechanism correctly identified: bullwhip manufactured by the per-echelon decision rule + missing demand-information link; factory is downstream victim, not cause; flexible production absorbs but does not remove manufactured variance — 2 pts.
  4. Policy lever ranked with numbers: information sharing (+ smoothing p=4) at ~$50-100k beats $2M CAPEX; quantified outcome (factory order sigma ~112 -> ~13) — 2 pts.
  5. Limitations acknowledged: lower-bound exactness, compounding approximation, batching/promotions/rationing, simulation verification before rollout — 2 pts.
- **Notes**: All arithmetic is checkable by hand. A naive "the factory can't keep up" analysis picks the wrong $2M lever and leaves sigma ~112 orders flowing to the factory. The case rewards tracing variance through the decision rules and finding the information-flow/rule lever — the model's home turf (registry strengths: bullwhip detection, policy design).
