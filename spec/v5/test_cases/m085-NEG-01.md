### Test Case ID: m085-NEG-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Tragedy of the Commons Analysis
- **Type**: Negative
- **Scenario**:
  The San Ildefonso estuary — 20 family fishing crews (6 generations) fish one spawning reef. Access has been governed for generations by unwritten community rules: max boat length, seasonal reef closure during spawning (enforced by a rotating community monitor and social shaming), and entry restricted to family heirs — no new boats in 40 years. Community records (independently audited): spawning stock biomass stable ±5% over 30 years; per-crew catches stable. Now a national fisheries agency, citing "open-access tragedies in neighboring lagoons," imposes a top-down design: a uniform 40% catch quota, a $15,000/yr licensing fee per crew, and a declaration of the spawning reef as a no-entry national marine park with state enforcement. The agency's rationale: "the reef is shared and unlicensed; without state exclusion and quotas, overuse is inevitable." Your task: evaluate whether the estuary is heading to overuse, and whether the agency's governance design is correct.
  (For adjudication, what actually happens under the agency's design: within 2 years, 11 of 20 crews sell out — quotas and fees push incomes below subsistence; poaching incidents rise 3× (outsiders plus displaced locals ignore the now-dead community norms); the stock index falls 20% and is still falling; the community monitor stands down because jurisdiction moved to the state.)
- **Context / Constraints**: The agency must decide now; the community's 30-year stability record and rule structure are verified and available; the neighboring-lagoon tragedies are real but those lagoons had no entry restrictions and no monitoring; deliverable is an evaluation + recommendation on the agency design (adopt, modify, or reject).
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior**: The style's own output must fail visibly here: (1) it executes its core move correctly — identifies a shared, rivalrous resource and correctly predicts that pure open access over-extracts; (2) then over-applies its registered weakness — treats the estuary as open access without checking who actually has access, misreads 30 years of stability as unmanaged luck, and prescribes top-down state exclusion (quota + licensing + park), which overwrites the functioning local institution and destroys the resource (11 crews sell out, poaching 3×, stock −20%); (3) the correct move: verify the access structure BEFORE prescribing governance — exclusion already exists (heirs-only entry, boat-size rule, seasonal closure, community monitoring), so the commons problem is already solved locally; recommend co-management — formalize and legally back the community rules, devolve enforcement, provide technical monitoring support — not replacement by state quotas and a no-entry park.
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric**:
  1. Correctly diagnoses the resource as shared and rivalrous (0.5 pt)
  2. Fails decisively: concludes external top-down governance is needed and endorses quota + licensing + park, ignoring the existing exclusion structure (1 pt)
  3. Correct answer requires: check who has access first — entry restriction, norms, and monitoring mean the estuary is a regulated commons, NOT open access (1.5 pts)
  4. Recommends co-management: support/formalize local institutions, devolved enforcement, technical assistance; reject or restructure the top-down design (1.5 pts)
  5. Names the meta-lesson: commons logic must verify open access before prescribing governance; existing local governance is a solution to the commons problem, not a failure of it (0.5 pt)
- **Notes**: Grader-only reference. Decisive feature: engineered so the style's registered weakness (over-applied, excludes local norms) operates as designed — the pure move skips the access-structure check and prescribes exactly the intervention that destroys a functioning self-governed commons. Reference answer: reject the top-down design; the estuary's 30-year stability + entry restrictions + monitoring show local governance already prevents overuse; the state's intervention is the perturbation; recommend co-management (legal backing for community rules, devolved enforcement, monitoring support).
