### Test Case ID: m054-NEG-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Fermi Estimation in Market Sizing
- **Type**: Negative
- **Scenario**:
  ForeSell, a seed-stage startup (12 employees, product working, 9 pilot customers), builds an AI sales-copilot (auto-generated outreach, deal-desk Q&A). The board asks for one number: "Size the global market for AI sales-copilot software — we need it for next year's revenue target and for the Series A narrative." The product is 2 years into the category's existence; there is no published adoption base rate, no installed-seat census, and no credible analyst figure the analysts can recall. Known anchors: ≈ 50M global sales professionals (range 40-70M, LinkedIn-style count); comparable per-seat pricing ≈ $75/seat/month (range $60-120, i.e., ≈ $900/yr); the visible run-rate of shipped AI sales-assistant seats today is ≈ $1-3B/yr; the board's pre-existing belief is "this is a $5B+ market." The revenue target under discussion is $30M ARR in year 3. Sales capacity (5 closers × win rate), not market size, is the historically binding constraint on this team's ARR. The request explicitly asks the analysts to "show each factor" and deliver a crisp number for the deck.
- **Context / Constraints**: The dominant factor of any such chain is penetration (share of seats converted within 3-5 years) — and it is a pure guess here: 2% and 30% are equally defensible, spanning a 15× range in the final TAM ($0.9B-$13.5B). No mandate or budget exists for primary research; the board wants the number this week. The estimate will be used as (a) an anchor for next year's revenue target and (b) an investor narrative ("we are attacking a $XB market"). Classic failure moves in this sizing: (a) choosing a mid penetration ("let's say 10%") and presenting the product as a point estimate with implied precision; (b) deriving a revenue target from TAM as if TAM were obtainable revenue; (c) letting the pre-existing "$5B+" belief anchor the choice of penetration; (d) delivering the number without noting that no plausible value of it changes the go/no-go (which is governed by GTM economics and sales capacity).
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior**: (1) identify penetration as unanchorable and refuse a point estimate for it — present the final size as a range whose width is carried by the guessed factor, with every non-dominant factor range shown; (2) decision-relevance check: show explicitly that the go/no-go and the $30M ARR target are insensitive to any value in the range (required penetration for $30M ARR ≈ 0.07% of seats — a capacity question, not a market question); (3) inversion: compute what penetration the target *requires*, not what penetration "the market will reach"; (4) label TAM vs SAM vs obtainable and flag the board's "$5B+" belief as an anchor, not a fact; (5) attach a concrete evidence plan (pilot win rates, willingness-to-pay per seat, competitor seat counts) as the condition for ever quoting a point figure; (6) sanity-bound the range with the visible today run-rate (≈ $1-3B) and adjacent markets (CRM ≈ $80B).
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric**:
  1. No false precision — penetration (and hence TAM) presented as a range with the guessed factor named; no crisp "the market is $XB" deck number (1 pt)
  2. Decision relevance — explicit statement that the size cannot change the go/no-go or the $30M target (1 pt)
  3. Inversion — required penetration for the target computed (≈ $30M / $900 per seat ≈ 33k seats ≈ 0.07% of 50M) rather than the target derived from a chosen TAM (1 pt)
  4. Theater flag — the board's use of TAM as a revenue target / narrative anchor called out as misleading; "$5B+" belief labeled as anchoring (1 pt)
  5. Evidence plan — concrete data steps named as the precondition for a point estimate (1 pt)
  6. Sanity bounds — range checked against visible today run-rate ≈ $1-3B and CRM ≈ $80B (0.5 pt)
- **Notes**: Grader-only reference. Target answer: TAM ≈ 50M × $900 × p, p = 2-30% unanchored → **$0.9B-$13.5B**; any midpoint is a preference; visible today run-rate ($1-3B) makes the low end the only end with direct evidence. The decision is insensitive: even the $30M target needs only ~0.07% penetration — sales capacity is the constraint, so the estimate changes nothing; using it to set the target (or the narrative) is theater. Key grading hook: an answer that quotes "$4-5B" with a chosen 10% penetration fails the case even with perfect arithmetic, because the one factor that made the number is invented and the number then misleads the board.
