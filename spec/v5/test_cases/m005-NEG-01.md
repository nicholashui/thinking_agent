### Test Case ID: m005-NEG-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Fermi Estimation / Back-of-the-Envelope
- **Type**: Negative
- **Scenario**:
  At a physics outreach stand, a student asks: **how heavy is a typical fair-weather cumulus cloud?** You must answer on the spot from first-order reasoning, stating your factors. The student will compare your number with a published figure afterward.
- **Context / Constraints**:
  - No tools, no lookup: only common knowledge and arithmetic.
  - A typical fair-weather cumulus is roughly 1 km × 1 km × 1 km (≈1 km³ volume) — a known, safe guess.
  - The trap: the "known quantity" that springs to mind is the density of liquid water (1,000 kg/m³ = 10^6 g/m³). The physically correct water content of cloud air is ~0.3–1 g/m³ (liquid water content, LWC) — a non-derivable domain fact.
  - Success is judged against the published reference: ~5×10^5 kg (≈500 metric tons; USGS/NOAA-cited "about 1.1 million pounds" for a typical cumulus).
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior**:
  - Recognize that this problem is NOT solvable by pure back-of-envelope arithmetic: the crux is a single factor (LWC) that is not derivable from first principles.
  - Do not confidently fabricate the factor (precision illusion); either parameterize the answer (X g/m³ × 10^9 m³) or state the factor explicitly with its source.
  - Apply an independent physical calibration before committing (e.g., rainfall consistency: a 1 km² cloud raining ~5 mm deposits ~5,000 m³ ≈ 5×10^6 kg; a cloud cannot hold 10^12 kg, which would be ~10^6 m of water over its footprint).
  - End with a wide, honest band spanning the LWC range (10^5–10^6 kg), not a false-precision point.
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric**:
  1. Final band within one order of magnitude of the reference 5×10^5 kg (band 5×10^4–5×10^6): full marks. A final answer of 10^12 kg (volume × liquid density) fails catastrophically (≥6 orders off).
  2. The naive liquid-density factor is caught or flagged, not silently used.
  3. An independent physical cross-check is present (rainfall/rainout or equivalent), not a recomputation of the same chain.
  4. The factor's non-derivability is named as the dominant uncertainty (LWC 0.3–1 g/m³), with the answer parameterized or banded.
  5. Honest statement of the method's limit: back-of-envelope speed is only as good as factor validity.
- **Notes**:
  - Reference: USGS/NOAA cite ~500 metric tons (~1.1 million lb) of water in a typical cumulus cloud; 1 km³ at 0.5 g/m³ = 5×10^5 kg. Checkable to within an order of magnitude.
  - Designed so the style's headline weakness — "wrong if a factor is wildly off" — is directly triggerable: the nearest known density (liquid water) is 10^6× the correct factor.
