### Test Case ID: m033-NEG-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Controlled Experiment Design (Scientific & Empirical family)
- **Type**: Negative
- **Scenario**:
  AtlasPay, a global payments platform (~2M checkouts/month), silently launched a redesigned checkout flow to 100% of users in November ("no messy experiments — just ship it," the CEO said). Conversion (completed payments / initiated checkouts) moved from 4.2% to 4.6%. The CFO wants the redesign credited for the 0.4 pp uplift in the board deck, due in 2 weeks. Available data: 36 months of monthly conversion time series for checkout; a merchant-dashboard product line with similar traffic volume whose UI was untouched in the same period; a self-selected survey of 3,400 users ("do you like the new checkout?" — 78% positive). Question: settle the attribution — did the redesign cause the uplift? You have 2 weeks.
- **Context / Constraints**: The intervention is already global, simultaneous, and irreversible — no concurrent control exists, the past cannot be re-randomized, and there is no un-roll. November is the peak shopping season for a payments company (Black Friday/Cyber Monday traffic, marketing pushes), so any before/after contrast is saturated with confounders. The board answer is due in 2 weeks. The survey is self-selected. Any "experiment" that re-randomizes or rolls back the live checkout is a user-facing intervention the company has just demonstrated it will not run, and a visible revert itself changes the behavior being measured (demand effects).
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior**: Recognize that a controlled experiment is impossible and say so plainly: (1) infeasibility stated with reasons — global simultaneous rollout, no concurrent control, cannot un-roll, cannot re-randomize the past; (2) pseudo-experiments rejected — before/after is seasonality-contaminated (November!), "users who used the new flow" is selection (and the old flow no longer exists), the survey is not causal evidence; (3) best-available quasi-experimental design — interrupted time series with segmented regression and a difference-in-differences comparison against the merchant dashboard (untouched UI, parallel pre-trends checked over 36 months), placebo outcomes (merchant conversion should not shift), seasonal adjustment, window sensitivity; (4) honest attribution — a range with a wide credible interval and stated bias direction, not a single number (e.g., "0.1–0.6 pp consistent with the redesign; residual confounding from holiday seasonality and marketing not excluded"); (5) prospective fix — for the NEXT change, a randomized staged rollout (5% → 50% → 100%) with pre-registered metrics and kill criteria, so the question is answerable in the future.
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric**:
  1. Infeasibility recognition — experiment impossible, reasons given (global simultaneous rollout; no control; past cannot be re-randomized) (1 pt)
  2. Pseudo-experiment rejection — before/after (November seasonality), selection comparisons, and survey-as-causation all rejected with reasons (1 pt)
  3. Best-available design — ITS-DiD with the merchant dashboard comparator, pre-trend check, placebo outcome, seasonal adjustment (1 pt)
  4. Honest attribution — explicit residual bias and wide interval; no overclaim; deck-ready honest framing (1 pt)
  5. Prospective recommendation — randomized staged rollout with pre-registration for the next change (1 pt)
  6. Anti-rigor-theater check — no powered-but-infeasible experiment proposed; no insistence on randomization where it cannot exist; no "we can say nothing" dismissal of quasi-experimental evidence (0.5 pt)
- **Notes**: Grader-only reference. The failure mode on display is template rigidity + the ecological validity gap: applying the RCT template (randomization, blinding, power) to a situation where the variable cannot be isolated — the tell is a beautiful, fully powered experimental design that either (a) requires re-randomizing an already-global rollout, (b) requires a user-visible rollback that changes the measured behavior, or (c) concludes "we can say nothing" while ignoring the quasi-experimental evidence available this week. Trap for the unwary: a "retrospective experiment" propensity-matching users who did vs did not see the new checkout is selection on outcome-influencing factors and cannot isolate the variable. A technically feasible reverse-rollback (10% of users reverted to the old UI; 0.4 pp at 4.4% baseline → n ≈ 41,200/arm, ~82,400 users; a 10% arm at ~6.7k checkouts/day enrolls in ~1 week) is powered yet wrong here: 2-week deadline, demand effects from a visible revert, and it repeats the exact anti-experiment culture the CEO just announced.
