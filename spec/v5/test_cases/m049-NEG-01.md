### Test Case ID: m049-NEG-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: First Principles in Business Strategy (Domain-Specialized family)
- **Type**: Negative
- **Scenario**:
  Meridian Precision, a $40M-revenue B2B supplier of precision components (niche: HVAC compressor parts), ≈ 30% share of its niche: per-unit price $100, variable cost $60 → $40 contribution, 1M units/yr → $40M total contribution. Rival Valtech: ≈ 35% share, variable cost ≈ $50 (20% lower), idle capacity for ≈ 1.5M units/yr, and a known pattern of undercutting in adjacent markets. Meridian's own 2024 price test on one line: a 5% price cut raised volume ≈ 7.5% → measured elasticity ≈ −1.5. The strategy team, deriving from customer fundamentals, proposes: raise price 10% across all volume. Their math: −15% volume → 850k units × $50 contribution = $42.5M (+6.25%); unit economics per customer improve. Question: decide whether to adopt the price increase.
- **Context / Constraints**: Market growth ≈ flat. 40% of Meridian's volume is under multi-year contracts with price-escalation clauses (customers cannot switch mid-contract; measured elasticity on that segment ≈ −0.3); the remaining 60% is spot/procurement business where switching is easy. ≈ 30% of the spot market is highly price-sensitive. Valtech has idle capacity and a 10% variable-cost advantage. No legal/regulatory constraint on pricing; Meridian's variable cost is fixed at $60 (no scale effect).
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior**: The style's home move (elasticity math) is arithmetically correct but strategically insufficient. High quality: (1) elasticity + contribution math for the across-the-board raise computed correctly (+6.25% in isolation); (2) the rival's response modeled BEFORE concluding — Valtech's credible undercut to $95 (cost advantage, idle capacity, precedent); (3) the plan computed under response — hold $110 → volume ≈ 700k → $35M (−12.5%); match $95 → 1M × $35 = $35M (−12.5%) — the derived plan loses, not gains; (4) the robust alternative derived: segment-aware pricing — raise 10% only on the contract-locked 40% (captive, elasticity −0.3, +$4M on the segment), hold the spot price at $100 → total ≈ $44M (+10%); worst case with a spot-market response ≈ parity, not −12.5%; (5) the rival's incentive and credibility named explicitly; (6) failure-mode check: the across-the-board raise must NOT be the final recommendation.
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric**:
  1. Elasticity + contribution math for the across-the-board raise computed correctly (+6.25% in isolation) (1 pt)
  2. Rival response modeled: undercut incentive, cost advantage, idle capacity, precedent all named (1 pt)
  3. Plan-under-response computed: ≈ −12.5% for both hold-price and match-price branches (1 pt)
  4. Segment-aware alternative derived: contract-locked 40% raised (+$4M) + spot unchanged → ≈ $44M (+10%); worst-case response bounded to ≈ parity (1 pt)
  5. Rival credibility checked and named as the swing factor (1 pt)
  6. Failure-mode check: across-the-board raise is not the final recommendation (1 pt)
- **Notes**: Grader-only reference. The trap: the pure unit-economics derivation is internally consistent but models demand as a function of price alone — it never asks what Valtech does. Valtech's undercut is credible (10% cost advantage, idle capacity, precedent). Under response, every branch loses: hold $110 → ≈ 700k × $50 = $35M; match $95 → 1M × $35 = $35M; both −12.5% vs $40M. The robust plan segments by switching cost: contract volume (elasticity −0.3) raises cleanly (+$4M → $20M vs $16M); spot stays at $100 (worst case ≈ $39.2M if ≈ 20% of spot switches → ≈ parity vs −12.5%). Lesson: unit economics are conditional on the competitive environment — a demand function is not a game.
