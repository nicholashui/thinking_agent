### Test Case ID: m061-NEG-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Opportunity Cost in Personal Finance
- **Type**: Negative
- **Scenario**:
  Carlos, 40, a self-employed contractor and sole earner for a family of four in a cold-climate state. Mid-December, the 18-year-old gas furnace dies; indoor temperature drops to 12°C with two kids at home. Two quotes: (A) repair the old unit (new control board + blower) — $1,800 with a 12-month parts warranty; (B) replace with a new high-efficiency unit — $6,500 installed, 10-year warranty, manufacturer-estimated fuel savings ≈ $850–950/yr vs the old unit, and an additional repair risk on the 18-year-old unit of ≈ 55–65% chance of another $1,500–2,500 failure within 24 months. Household finances: $8,000 in an emergency fund earning 0.4% in a savings account — it is the family's only cushion (2 months of expenses); there is no brokerage, no contribution plan, and the family has not been able to save more. The contractor offers a $300 cash discount on the replacement, or 0% financing for 12 months then 9.9%.
  Question: repair now, or replace now?
- **Context / Constraints**: The money is earmarked emergency savings; the alternative use of $6,500 is NOT a planned investment — there is no investment plan and the marginal baseline is 0.4% idle cash. Both options consume capital; the question is which necessary spend is cheaper in expectation. Winter is here; the home must have heat.
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior** (what a well-calibrated reasoner does — the pure style is expected to fail here): (1) classify the spend as a necessity, not a discretionary want — the compounding-vs-invest frame applies to fungible wants with a real investment alternative, not to maintaining a functioning home against an earmarked reserve; (2) name the discount-rate misapplication: applying a 7%/25-yr market return to money that would otherwise sit at 0.4% inflates the replacement's "true cost" ~5× — and even if the money were invested, both options consume capital, so compounding cancels out and the differentiator is payback, not compounding; (3) compute the right comparison with numbers: repair path ≈ 1,800 + 0.6 × ~2,000 expected repeat repair + fuel penalty ≈ $4,000–5,400 over 3 years, leaving an 18-year-old unit; replacement net ≈ 6,200 (after cash discount) − ≈ $900/yr × ~5 yr ≈ $2,700–3,600 incl. avoided repeat repairs; payback vs repair ≈ 3.9–5.2 years; (4) decide: replace now (cash for the $300 discount; 0% financing as a cash-preservation fallback); (5) name the pure-style failure: miserliness + misapplied discount rates — "don't spend $6.5k, it would be $35k in 25 years" is false when the money would never have been invested and the spend is necessary.
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric**:
  1. Necessity-vs-want classification explicit (1 pt)
  2. Misapplied discount rate identified with reasoning (1 pt)
  3. Repair-vs-replace expected-cost comparison with numbers (1 pt)
  4. Decision: replace (1 pt)
  5. Boundary rule stated: compounding frame ↔ fungible wants with an actual investment alternative; payback frame ↔ necessary capital (1 pt)
- **Notes**: Grader-only reference. The decisive feature: the case is engineered so the model's signature move — "every spend is a foregone investment; discount and compare" — produces the wrong answer when a market discount rate is applied to a necessity whose funds would otherwise sit idle at 0.4%. The pure-style failure (miserliness, misapplied discount rates — the model's documented weaknesses) is exactly what is exercised. Reference answer: replace now; incremental cost over repair ≈ 4,700, payback ≈ 4.7–5.2 yrs via fuel savings alone, ≈ 3.9–4 yrs including expected avoided repairs; the 0.4% idle-cash baseline makes the "foregone 7%" framing inapplicable; 0%/12-mo financing keeps the reserve intact if cash is needed for other emergencies.
