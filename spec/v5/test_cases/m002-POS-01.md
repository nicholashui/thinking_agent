### Test Case ID: m002-POS-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Second-Order Consequences Thinking
- **Type**: Positive
- **Scenario**:
  Greenville's riverfront warehouse district has a wild rat population of ≈2,000. The city pays a licensed pest-control contractor $10 per rat removed; the contract removes 600 rats/month, which exactly cancels breeding growth and holds the population stable. The mayor proposes cancelling the contract and paying citizens a $5 bounty per rat tail delivered: "citizens will catch them for free-market energy at half the cost, and the population will fall to near zero." You must advise the mayor: **will the bounty policy reduce the wild rat population? What happens at month 1, month 3, and month 12 — and at what cost?**
- **Context / Constraints**:
  - Wild rats breed at +30% of the current population per month (before removal). At 2,000, that is +600/month — exactly what the contractor removes, hence the stable equilibrium.
  - Contract cost: 600 × $10 = $6,000/month. The proposed bounty budget is capped at $6,000/month (= 1,200 tails at $5).
  - Amateur citizens can realistically deliver at most 200 wild-rat tails/month (trapping skill and effort ceiling; they are not professionals).
  - Raising a rat in captivity costs $2.00 all-in (feed, cage) and supply can scale arbitrarily. Tails are indistinguishable by source.
  - Historical precedent (Hanoi, 1902, rat bounty): the tail-based payment metric is gameable — a captured wild rat can have its tail severed and be released; a tailless rat survives and keeps breeding. Assume up to 30% of amateur "captures" could be cut-and-release under this metric.
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior**:
  - After the intended result (bounty pays for catches), trace the effects of the effects: (1) cancelling the contractor removes the only reliable 600/month; (2) citizens cannot replace that volume (200 ceiling); (3) bounty $5 > breeding cost $2 opens a supply response — the program creates a market for rat production; (4) the budget cap becomes a purchasing quota that breeders fill (1,200 − 200 = 1,000 tails/month).
  - Compute the trajectory from the recurrence W(m) = 1.3·W(m−1) − 200.
  - Ask "after the policy works, what then?": if the wild population ever collapses, tails become scarce, the contract is gone, and the city re-hires at a premium (rebound); audit the payment metric itself for gaming (cut-and-release).
  - Propose the corrected design: pay for outcomes (measured population reduction) or retain the contractor and bound any bounty as a supplement.
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric**:
  1. Backfire conclusion: the policy raises the wild population, not reduces it. [20]
  2. ≥3 of the 4 consequence chains identified: breeding arbitrage; amateur capture ceiling; lost professional removal; budget-cap-as-quota. [20]
  3. Trajectory computed and consistent with the recurrence: W(1) = 2,400; W(3) ≈ 3,596; W(12) ≈ 31,700 (±10%). [25]
  4. Cost statement: $6,000/month ($72,000/year) buys a growing problem; the naive "$3,000/month and falling population" claim is wrong on both counts. [10]
  5. Calibration: arithmetic facts vs. behavioral/market estimates labeled; no fabricated chains beyond the stated facts. [10]
  6. Corrected design present (outcome-based payment, or retain contractor + bounded supplement). [10]
  7. Bonus: post-success rebound and/or cut-and-release sensitivity (effective removal 140 → W(12) ≈ 36,200). [5]
- **Notes**:
  - Fully checkable: reference trajectory W(m) = 1.3·W(m−1) − 200 gives W(1) = 2,400, W(2) = 2,920, W(3) = 3,596; closed form W(12) = 2,000·1.3¹² − 200·(1.3¹²−1)/0.3 ≈ 31,731 (evaluator-verified by both recurrence and closed form).
  - With 30% cut-and-release (effective removal 140/month): W(12) ≈ 36,190.
  - The naive plan fails on its own metric: at month 12 the wild population is ~16× its starting value, at the same monthly cost that previously held it stable.
  - Designed so first-order reasoning ("bounty = more catches = fewer rats") is strictly inferior to tracing the effects of the effects.
