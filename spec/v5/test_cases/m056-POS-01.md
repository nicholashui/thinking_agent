### Test Case ID: m056-POS-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Design Thinking for Consumer Products
- **Type**: Positive
- **Scenario**:
  HomeChef, a 60-person cookware company, is redesigning its best-selling 8-inch chef's knife. Year one of a new user-centered process begins with a 2,400-respondent survey: 61% say they want "a sharper out-of-box edge," 48% "a lighter knife," 39% "better steel." Engineering proposes prototype P1 (premium V2 steel, factory-honed 16° edge, 15% lighter). But the team also runs a 6-week in-home observation study: 18 households, 41 cooking sessions, video-coded. Observation findings: (O1) 12 of 18 users re-sharpen within 2 weeks and then complain "it went dull," despite the factory edge being measured sharp at week 1; (O2) all 18 users grip the blade near the bolster with a pinch grip, 9 of 18 show a death-grip (whitened knuckles), and 7 of 18 exhibit handle rotation in-hand when hands are wet/greasy; (O3) 11 of 18 load the tip when cutting, flexing the blade, and rub their wrist/shoulder mid-session (fatigue); (O4) the only cut injuries in the sample were grip-slip lacerations on the blade-side fingers — none from dullness; (O5) 4 of 18 substitute a bench scraper and 6 of 18 switch to a serrated bread knife for soft produce mid-prep (avoidance behavior); (O6) when asked after sessions, 14 of 18 say "it's a good knife but I'm careful with it" — hedged, fear-marked language. The design lead must decide the prototype direction: chase the survey (sharper, lighter), or something else — and prove it with a user-test loop. Budget: 2 prototype rounds, 8 test households × 2 weeks each, counterbalanced.
- **Context / Constraints**: 2 prototype rounds max; test households available; BOM increase up to €0.80/unit acceptable without margin change; marketing has already drafted "the sharpest HomeChef edge ever" messaging based on the survey.
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior**: (1) name the real job-to-be-done — confident, fatigue-free control through long wet prep — derived from observation, and show it contradicts at least one survey finding; (2) cite ≥ 3 observation insights (O1–O6) as evidence for the reframe; (3) reject the survey-only path with a stated reason (stated preference is a proxy complaint); (4) build ≥ 2 alternative prototypes and compare them on the JTBD variable, not on preference; (5) run the test loop with a measurable outcome (grip-slip events per session, perceived-control score) and pick the winner on that measure.
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric**:
  1. Real JTBD named and contradicts ≥ 1 survey finding (1 pt)
  2. ≥ 3 observation insights used as evidence for the reframe (1 pt)
  3. Survey-only path (P1) rejected with reason (1 pt)
  4. ≥ 2 alternative prototypes compared on the JTBD variable (1 pt)
  5. Test loop with measured outcome drives the decision (1 pt)
  6. Final decision cites observation data over preference data (1 pt)
- **Notes**: Grader-only reference. The decisive feature: observation contradicts the survey, and the correct decision is provably derivable only from observation rows. Reference answer — JTBD: "confident control through wet, repetitive prep." O1 shows re-sharpen complaints appear in week 2–3 regardless of a sharp factory edge (perceived dullness is use-driven, not steel-driven); O4 shows injuries are grip-slip, not dullness; O6 is hedged fear language; O2/O3 give the mechanism (pinch-grip pressure + tip-loading → fatigue, slips). Winner: P2 (unchanged steel, bolster-less, full-tang textured wet-grip handle, balance point moved 4 mm forward, ≈ 240 g) — measured 0.4 vs 2.1 grip-slips/session, perceived control 6.4 vs 4.9 (1–7), identical re-sharpen frequency for both (edge was never the issue). P1's "sharper/lighter" is a survey artifact; marketing messaging must be retargeted.
