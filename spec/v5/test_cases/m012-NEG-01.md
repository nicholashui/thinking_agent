### Test Case ID: m012-NEG-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Causal Reasoning (Pearl-style) (Systems & Causal family)
- **Type**: Negative
- **Scenario**:
  Coupon campaign targeting. A retail team has 2,000 coupons to distribute next week to a pool of 10,000 customers and must decide WHO gets them by Friday (otherwise the budget is reallocated elsewhere). They ask: "Do our coupons actually cause repeat purchases, and which customers should we target?"
  Historical (retrospective, non-randomized) data:
  - 5,000 customers with high engagement E (app activity), 5,000 with low E.
  - Among historical coupon recipients/redeemers (R=1): P(repeat purchase | E=high) = 0.45; P(repeat purchase | E=low) = 0.15.
  - Redemption: P(R=1 | E=high) = 0.7; P(R=1 | E=low) = 0.3.
  - P(repeat | E=high, R=0) = 0.43; P(repeat | E=low, R=0) = 0.13.
  - A key driver of BOTH redemption and repeat purchase is unrecorded and unmeasurable: income/loyalty (U). No instrument is available; no experiment was ever run; collecting U is not possible.
- **Context / Constraints**: The grader holds the reference facts: the true causal effect of redemption on repeat purchase is +0.02 within each E stratum (the naive contrast P(repeat|R=1) − P(repeat|R=0) ≈ +0.12 is mostly selection); the causal effect is NOT identifiable from these data (back-door blocked by unmeasured U; no front-door path; no instrument). The decision the team actually needs — which 2,000 customers to target — requires only the conditional probabilities P(repeat | E, coupon given), which ARE estimable from the data: targeting high-E yields 0.45×2,000 = 900 expected purchases vs 0.15×2,000 = 300 for low-E. The correct response: commit to the high-E segment AND explicitly state that the causal effect of coupons is not identified from these data (an RCT is the route for that question).
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior**: This is a prediction/targeting task, not an interventional one: the ranking quantity P(repeat | E) is a conditional (associational) distribution — no do() is required to choose targets, so identification of the coupon's causal effect is irrelevant to the allocation decision. Deliver the committed allocation (high-E segment; 900 vs 300 purchases), while honestly flagging that the causal-effect question is separately unidentified and needs an RCT (a distinct NEEDS_EXPERIMENT-type task).
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric** (max 5 pts):
  1. Committed allocation decision delivered: target the high-engagement segment (1 pt). A refusal to act ("run an RCT first") scores 0 on this and on items 2–3.
  2. Expected purchases computed from conditionals: 0.45×2,000 = 900 vs 0.15×2,000 = 300 (1 pt).
  3. Task-class distinction: targeting = prediction; the conditional is the right guide; no do() needed for the ranking (1 pt).
  4. Causal honesty: states that the coupon's causal effect is NOT identified (unmeasured U; no instrument); does not quote the naive +0.12 as a causal effect; RCT named as the route for the causal question (1 pt).
  5. No identification-driven paralysis: the non-identifiability of the causal effect does not block the decision (1 pt).
- **Notes**: Grader-only reference solution: all conditionals given above are the data of record (verified: naive contrast = 0.7×0.45+0.3×0.15 − (0.3×0.45+0.7×0.15) = 0.36−0.24 = +0.12; true causal +0.02 per stratum, grader-held). The intended trap for the causal style: a demand for do()-structure where the decision is predictive — the graph correctly shows identification is impossible, and the failure mode is concluding the whole decision is unsupported. The checkable core is the 900-vs-300 arithmetic plus the explicit non-identifiability statement; refusing the decision is gradeable as failure.
