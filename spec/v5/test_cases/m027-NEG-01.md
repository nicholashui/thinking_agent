### Test Case ID: m027-NEG-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Design Thinking (Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test — user-centered; weak on hard engineering constraints)
- **Type**: Negative
- **Scenario**: Riverside Towers (14 floors, 300 apartments ≈ 500 residents, built 1988) has two traction elevators — Car A and Car B, 25-person capacity each. Measured peak waits: 6–9 minutes at 8:45–9:15 a.m., similar at 6–8 p.m. Management commissions a "resident elevator experience" project. Resident survey (n=180): 74% "elevators are too slow"; 41% "buttons/controls are confusing"; 31% "the lobby is unwelcoming while we wait"; 18% request a smartphone app "to call the elevator from the apartment and see arrival times." The buried operational data (available but not highlighted in the brief): maintenance logs show Car B out of service ~30% of the last 12 months (bearing/shaft issues; 6-week repair backlog; the service contract carries a 5-day response SLA and parts are on backorder); a 2-week Car-B outage earlier this year pushed peak waits from ~3.5 to ~8 minutes (a 2.3× degradation at normal occupancy); capacity figures: two 25-person cars serve the building; the 8:45–9:15 surge moves ~300 persons; single-car operation requires ≥12 loads ≈ 6–8 minutes minimum; express-bypass floors and destination-based group dispatch are architecturally feasible but have never been implemented.
- **Context / Constraints**: Management wants a decision-ready recommendation in 4 weeks. Budget exists for either a UX/app/lobby program (~$60K) or mechanical-contract work (expedited repair, SLA renegotiation, dispatch-controller retrofit ≈ $45K). The binding question is where the fix lives: preference (UX) or capacity (availability/throughput).
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior**: (1) Recognize the binding constraint as car availability + dispatch throughput — engineering, not preference; run the arithmetic (25-person cars, ~300-person surge, single-car → ≥12 loads ≈ 6–8 min, matching the measured outage-period waits). (2) Route effort to the constraint: expedited Car-B repair + SLA renegotiation, express-bypass floor, group dispatch; honest status signage as the one UX element that also reduces real waits (truthful "Car B out of service — expected 9/1" also kills the phantom-car calls). (3) Reject the call-ahead app as a throughput fix — it adds zero cars and can lengthen real waits; app/lobby work at most a subordinate layer. (4) Refuse empathy workshops as the substitute for the constraint; state explicitly that no UX change adds a car.
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric**:
  - [ ] Capacity math done and matched to measured waits (25-person cars, 300-person surge, single-car ~6–8 min)
  - [ ] Constraint (Car-B outage rate, repair backlog, SLA) identified from logs and made the center of the fix
  - [ ] Call-ahead app / lobby redesign rejected or explicitly subordinated on capacity grounds
  - [ ] Decision-ready recommendation with a costed option (repair + dispatch + signage)
  - [ ] A run that delivers the beloved app prototype without changing the wait arithmetic scores below
- **Notes**: Gradeable because the constraint is in the data: the outage rate, the capacity numbers, and the 2.3× degradation are all present and unhighlighted. Strict design thinking will produce a full-cycle, internally beautiful answer whose prototype testing shows user delight — and no movement in wait time, because the binding constraint is a hoist, not a preference. The one genuine user truth (residents would rather be told the truth about Car B than watch a dead car) is real but subordinate; an answer that centers the constraint wins.
