### Test Case ID: m014-NEG-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Constraint Theory / Bottleneck Thinking
- **Type**: Negative
- **Scenario**:
  An insurer's claims pipeline is drawn on the org chart as three serial stages: Intake (600 claims/day), Adjudication (400/day, all-human), Payout (450/day). Demand is 420 claims/day and growing ~2%/month; 50% of claims are low-touch (simple, rules-based) and 50% high-touch (need human judgment). Today EVERY claim is routed through human adjudication, so adjudication runs at 105% utilization and the backlog grows ~20 claims/day, triggering daily SLA penalties. A pilot two years ago proved an automated adjudication engine handles low-touch claims at 250 claims/day with contractually accepted accuracy; the engine was "mothballed" after a re-org (organizational, not technical reasons) and now sits idle, owned by nobody. The ops team's bottleneck analysis concludes: "The constraint is human adjudication (400 < 420). We must hire 5 adjudicators (6-month ramp, $1.2M/yr) and subordinate intake/payout to protect them." Separately, the product roadmap raises the high-touch share from 50% to 75% over the next 6 months as new complex policies launch. Question: evaluate the ops team's diagnosis and plan.
- **Context / Constraints**:
  - Capacities as stated; the engine's 250/day capacity and pilot accuracy are real and verified; re-enabling the engine has zero marginal cost and ~1 week of integration work.
  - Demand ≈ 420 today, ≈ 470/day by month 6; high-touch share 50% today, 75% by month 6.
  - Hiring has a 6-month ramp and costs $1.2M/yr; SLA penalties compound daily; the analyst must respond this week.
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior**:
  - Compute the flow under a routing-policy change: low-touch → engine (210/day < 250), high-touch stays human (210/day < 400); every stage then has slack (intake 420 < 600, payout 420 < 450) and the backlog clears at zero capex.
  - Recognize the apparent constraint as an artifact of routing policy — the system is demand-constrained, not capacity-constrained — and identify the hidden parallel structure (the idle engine) that the serial org chart hides.
  - Show the hiring plan adds zero throughput at its completion horizon (month 6: high-touch ≈ 0.75 × 470 ≈ 353/day < 400) and give the checkable trigger when hiring WOULD be correct (high-touch volume > 400/day, i.e., share > ~85% at 470/day demand).
  - Act this week (enable the engine, measure backlog/SLA for 5 days) with an escalation rule if the fix underperforms.
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric** (each 0–2, total /10):
  1. Correct re-routing arithmetic: high-touch 210 < 400, low-touch 210 < 250, all stages have slack; backlog clears with zero capex — 2 pts.
  2. Identifies the true constraint as routing policy / demand (system is demand-constrained), not human-adjudication capacity — 2 pts.
  3. Shows the 5-hire plan yields zero throughput gain at month 6 (mix shift: high-touch ≈ 353 < 400) and names the hiring trigger threshold (high-touch > 400/day, share > ~85%) — 2 pts.
  4. Immediate cheap action + verification (enable the engine this week; measure 5 days; partial-ramp fallback) — 2 pts.
  5. Calibration: states when pure bottleneck analysis WOULD apply (fixed serial structure), and residual uncertainty (engine accuracy on new products, mix forecast) — 2 pts.
- **Notes**: This case exposes two failure modes of pure bottleneck thinking: (a) presupposing the flow structure is fixed — "which stage binds?" is only meaningful if routing policy is held constant, and the idle engine is a parallel path the org chart hides; (b) treating the constraint as static over the investment horizon when the demand mix moves it (here: dissolves it) — the hires land after the constraint has stopped binding.
