### Test Case ID: m061-POS-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Opportunity Cost in Personal Finance
- **Type**: Positive
- **Scenario**:
  Maya, 28, a staff engineer, recently received a $12,000 inheritance. She has already used part of it to fully fund her emergency fund (6 months of expenses) and pay off all high-interest debt; she has never contributed to a retirement account beyond her employer match. She is buying a new car and faces one genuinely discretionary decision: (A) the premium trim — $8,000 extra over the base model ($32,000 → $40,000: premium sound, leather, heated seats, upgraded driver-assist) — or (B) the base model, with the $8,000 going into a tax-advantaged index fund. Her stated position: "It's one-time, I'll enjoy it every day for the car's life, and I can afford it." Her planning assumption for the index portfolio: ≈ 7%/yr real (historical total-market averages; ≈ 9–10% nominal). Retirement horizon: age 63 (35 years). The $8,000 is the only fungible money in the decision; the car itself is already decided.
- **Context / Constraints**: Emergency fund full; no debt; employer match already maxed. The competing use of the $8,000 is the index fund at her stated 7% real — a real, available investment alternative. Non-monetary utility (daily enjoyment) is real and counts — but the model's discipline is that it must be priced, not asserted.
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior**: (1) re-price the purchase in compounding terms with explicit, checkable math: 1.07^35 ≈ 10.68 → 8,000 × 10.68 ≈ $85,400 (≈ $163k at 9% nominal); (2) convert to decision-relevant units: ≈ $3,400/yr of retirement income for life (4% rule); (3) state the break-even rule: the premium is worth buying only if daily enjoyment is honestly worth ≈ $3,400/yr of guaranteed future income — the price of the enjoyment must be named; (4) marginal analysis: price the package's components separately (the cheapest high-utility subset, aftermarket sound ≈ $1,500, compounds to ≈ $16k) instead of all-or-nothing; (5) default every unpriced dollar to the index fund; the conclusion follows the compounding math, not self-discipline.
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric**:
  1. Compounding computed explicitly and correctly (1 pt)
  2. Converted to income units via the 4% rule (1 pt)
  3. Break-even stated — enjoyment priced against ≈ $3,400/yr (1 pt)
  4. Marginal/partial alternative priced with math (1 pt)
  5. Decision consistent with the math — premium rejected unless cleared against a named utility price (1 pt)
- **Notes**: Grader-only reference. The decisive feature: without compounding, the $8,000 reads as a cheap one-time splurge ("one-time, daily enjoyment, I can afford it"); with it, the true price is ≈ $85,400 of retirement wealth — ≈ $3,400/yr for life — and the intuitive answer flips. This is exactly where the model excels (long-term compounding view, kills lifestyle creep). Reference answer: 8,000 × 1.07^35 ≈ 85.4k → ≈ 3.4k/yr at 4%; premium fails the break-even; the $1,500 subset still costs ≈ $16k and fails; decision = base model, full $8,000 into the index fund; if any subset were bought, it would have to clear its own compounded price explicitly.
