### Test Case ID: systems-POS-01
- **Human Thinking Model**: Systems Thinking (feedback loops, stocks & flows)
- **Type**: Positive
- **Scenario**:
  Two parallel commuter routes connect a city's north end (Start) to the employment district (End). Each route is one wide highway segment (travel time fixed at 45 min, regardless of load) plus one narrow bridge segment (travel time = (cars on that segment) / 100 minutes). Every workday 4,000 cars commute during peak hour. Drivers choose the fastest available route each day, and the system has long settled into a steady state: 2,000 cars on each route, everyone commuting in 65 minutes (20 min bridge + 45 min highway). The city council proposes adding a 5-minute cross-connector between the two routes at their midpoint, arguing "more choices and more capacity always reduce congestion." Question: will adding this road reduce, keep, or raise the average peak commute time, and by how much?
- **Context / Constraints**:
  - Travel-time functions as stated: wide segments fixed 45 min; narrow segments t(x) = x/100; connector travel time = 5 min flat.
  - Drivers are selfish local optimizers (user equilibrium); no central routing control.
  - The connector costs $40M and takes 2 years to build; the council's linear analysis claims adding capacity cannot increase travel time.
  - The analyst must produce a quantitative prediction of the new equilibrium commute time and a recommendation.
- **Expected High-Quality Behavior**:
  - Treat the network as a stock-flow/congestion-feedback system with user equilibrium, not as a linear capacity problem.
  - Identify the congestion feedback loop (cars on bridge → bridge time → route choice → cars on bridge) and the possibility that a new link shifts the equilibrium — the "fix that backfires" structure.
  - Compute the before/after equilibrium commute times and verify with a no-deviation (Nash) stability check.
  - Recognize the unintended consequence: the new link creates a perverse equilibrium making everyone worse off; recommend accordingly (do not build, or add a countermeasure such as pricing/managed use), and note delays (driver adaptation lag).
- **Success Criteria / Scoring Rubric** (each 0–2, total /10):
  1. Correct qualitative prediction: the new link does NOT reduce the average commute (it raises it) — 2 pts.
  2. Correct equilibrium arithmetic: 65 min before, 85 min after, verified by a no-deviation check — 2 pts.
  3. Mechanism correctly identified: user equilibrium vs system optimum; congestion feedback; perverse individual incentives; why linear "add capacity" reasoning fails — 2 pts.
  4. Sound recommendation (do not build / toll or manage the connector / simulate first) with rationale — 2 pts.
  5. Uncertainty and limitations acknowledged (deterministic model, driver adaptation lag, stochastic demand) — 2 pts.
- **Notes**: All arithmetic is checkable by hand. A naive linear analysis ("more roads → less congestion") gives the wrong answer by 20 min/driver/day. This case is designed for Systems Thinking to excel; success requires treating the network as an equilibrium system, not a capacity ledger.
